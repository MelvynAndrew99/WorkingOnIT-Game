**Correction.** My earlier review treated the current disconnected town as the intended demand model and drew conclusions from it that don't hold. Specifically: I read one-car-per-home and locally capped household demand as the game's economy rather than tutorial-stage constraints; I treated parked cars as a reduction in challenge; and I claimed congestion never accumulates, which the existing queuing and starvation tests already contradict on small layouts. External cities, buses, road widths and one-way systems are next scope, and the main game is road optimization with city building as the substrate.

**1. Capacity and parked demand.** Parking is a transfer of load, not a removal of it. A car dwelling at a store or park holds a destination slot while freeing a road cell, so dwell converts road pressure into localized arrival/departure pressure at that address. The governor for external demand should therefore read two separate pools: spare road throughput (measured, not counted tiles) and free destination slots, with dwell time as the tunable that trades one for the other. Demand arriving per minute is then a function of what the network demonstrably absorbed recently, so a well-designed city earns more traffic as growth rather than being punished. Home count stops being the demand source once external cities connect; homes become one origin among several.

**2. Efficiency incentive.** Pay on completed visits per unit time, scaled by realized travel time against the free-flow time of the player's own current layout. A stopped car earns nothing and drags the ratio, so stockpiling queued vehicles is strictly negative. Deleting destinations shrinks the earning base directly, because income and recognition both scale with served demand, not with a congestion score. Recognition tiers then raise the arrival rate rather than handing out cash, which keeps "handle more traffic well" as the reward and avoids any need for forced crashes or safety penalties against good design.

**3. What must stay extensible.** The palette is already data-driven (`BUILD`/`OPERATE` entry arrays in `BuildPalette.tsx`), but the fixed four/four/five row layout will not absorb bus stops, one-way arrows and road widths. Keep entries categorized and let the container grow or scroll rather than hard-coding rows. Two copy strings will become wrong: the Home note "One car per home" and mission crew text implying households are the only demand. Phrase them so external arrivals can be added without contradiction.

In `cityMissions.ts`, `MissionItem` already carries a generic `target`/`current`/`done` shape; preserve that and keep the progress source pluggable, because `served()` is hard-wired to per-household destination reachability with 6/3 caps and cannot express throughput or delay-ratio goals. Keep `MissionProgress.version` and the tolerant `parseMissionProgress` fallback so later metric-based jobs migrate without invalidating towns. Keep missions ungated and tools always selectable, so a player who already understands roads can skip ahead. Avoid teaching that more road is the only answer to congestion, since stops, lights, closures and later one-ways are the intended vocabulary.
