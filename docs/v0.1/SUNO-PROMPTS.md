# AI Overlord: Suno music prompts

These are creative prompts, not generated audio or approved music. The user can generate candidates externally in Suno and bring back the results for listening and selection. No audio generation or game integration has been performed.

This is a proposed direction because detailed music preferences have not been established. Start with the baseline prompt and select its feel before refining the other cues.

## Shared musical identity

Playful office funk meets compact electronic game music: dry electric piano, rounded synth bass, woody mallet notes, and lightly swung drum-machine grooves. Memorable classic-game personality with modern clarity, without requiring chiptune or copying a named artist or soundtrack. The joke is the music's calmly competent manner while the work gets ridiculous.

Use a sparse four-note signature: C, E, D, G, with two short notes, one slightly longer note, a short answer, then a rest. Repeat this description across prompts. Separate generations may not reproduce the same melody, timing, key, or sound; select compatible takes by ear. This is a proposed musical seed, not a claim of exclusivity or verified originality in generated output.

Tempo, duration, and loop-friendly phrasing are requested targets, not guarantees. These are plain creative descriptions, not special parameter syntax or claims about current Suno controls.

No ominous villain atmosphere, aggressive bass, huge build, or sudden volume jump.

## Listening and selection

- Generate the baseline first. The user and son choose the music; these prompts are not an approved style decision.
- Listen alongside gameplay at low music volume, on a phone speaker and headphones. Honks, release confirmations, and rule-change cues must remain distinguishable.
- Compare the same short shift with quiet music and with music off. Check concentration, retry fatigue, and whether chaos stays funny. Household feedback is useful, not proof of a general player effect.
- Listen through several repeats. Check loop seams, stray vocal sounds, abrupt level changes, and melody fatigue. Verify the requested duration, tempo, and instrumentation in actual audio.
- Do not make music louder or harsher because a player makes a mistake. If overload music is adopted, associate it with sustained workload and restrained transitions, not punishment for each crash.
- These are separate candidate cues, not synchronized stems. Matching requested BPM and key does not guarantee seamless transitions. One good baseline track is sufficient for the first music pass; adaptive layering is not required.
